We measure what people do in the two seconds before a crash,
and build vehicles that expect it.

Pedestrian safety research at the School of Vehicle and Mobility, Tsinghua University. Our work runs as one loop: measure real human reactions in safety-critical scenarios, turn them into road-user models with realistic dynamic behavior, and use those models to design and validate AI-driven integrated safety systems that reduce injury.

Why this matters

Pedestrians are about a quarter of all road deaths, and the numbers are not falling as fast as vehicle-occupant fatalities. Automated vehicles promise to help, but their safety logic mostly assumes pedestrians keep walking at constant speed. Real people don't: in the last two seconds they run, freeze, or step back, and that reaction decides whether a crash happens and how badly they are hurt.

Crash databases record the injury but not the reaction. That missing two seconds is the gap our group works in.

Three research directions, one loop

Each direction answers a question the next one depends on. Together they take a pedestrian's reaction from measurement, through modeling, to a protection system that acts before impact.

1

Pedestrian behavior and pedestrian–vehicle interaction

Question: how do people actually perceive, decide, and move when a vehicle is about to hit them, and how does that reaction change the crash?

Others study crossing decisions in normal traffic from cameras and drones. We put volunteers into immersive, safety-critical VR conflicts with full-body motion capture and EEG, so we can measure the perception–decision–execution timeline that crash databases never contain.

  • Pedestrians who avoid a collision decide in 0.17–0.24 s versus 0.41 s for those who are hit; from that we derived a distance-based safety envelope for vehicle–pedestrian conflicts.
  • Decision uncertainty first falls, then rises again as urgency grows; EEG shows a shift to top-down processing under high urgency, which is when pedestrians become hardest to predict.
  • Under rising spatiotemporal pressure pedestrians move through three decision modes: no-risk, game, and life-saving; a model built on these modes predicts whether and how they avoid with precision 0.87 / 0.82.
  • The same behavior-to-injury logic extends to other vulnerable road users: e-scooter riders and cyclists share posture and kinematics differences that flip their head-injury risk depending on vehicle front-end shape.
  • Nie et al., Scientific Reports, 2021
  • Li et al., Cell Reports Physical Science, 2025
  • Liu et al., Accident Analysis & Prevention, 2026
  • Huang et al., Accident Analysis & Prevention, 2020
2

Road user modeling with realistic dynamic behaviors

Question: can a virtual pedestrian perceive risk, decide, and move like a real one, so that vehicle testing and prediction stop assuming constant-speed walking?

Most pedestrian models and trajectory predictors are trained on low-risk public datasets where avoidance behavior barely exists. Ours are built directly on the safety-critical behavior data from Direction 1, including age-specific avoidance ability.

  • A digital pedestrian with probabilistic risk perception, avoidance decision, and kinematic response, implemented in CARLA and validated against real near-crash trajectories; across 4,200 interactions, modeled avoidance cut collisions by 27.8% relative to no-avoidance baselines.
  • A transformer trajectory predictor fine-tuned with reinforcement learning from a human-designed reward model, improving prediction of rare avoidance behaviors (brierFDE −11.2%) while keeping accuracy on common ones; dataset released.
  • A kinematics-and-physiology scaling method that maps young volunteers' avoidance speed to middle-aged and older pedestrians, giving age-specific potential-collision areas without exposing elderly subjects to risk.
  • Liu et al., IEEE Intelligent Transportation Systems Magazine, 2026
  • Gao et al., Accident Analysis & Prevention, 2026
  • Liu et al., Traffic Injury Prevention, 2025
3

AI-driven integrated safety and injury prevention

Question: once a vehicle can see the risk coming, how should active control and passive protection act together, and when, to minimise injury rather than just collisions?

Active and passive pedestrian safety have been developed as separate modules with separate metrics. We connect them into one risk-to-injury chain: perception-based risk assessment triggers braking, steering, and deployable protection on a single timeline, evaluated by injury outcome and validated on real fleet data.

  • Technology: from 23 reconstructed real crashes, an intelligent safety system triggered at TTC −0.5 s reduces the probability of severe (AIS3+) head injury by 59%, with activation thresholds derived for AEB, AES, and passive devices.
  • System: a prediction-based trigger for active hoods gains a nominal 200 ms before impact, enough for reversible electric actuation and up to 62% HIC reduction at 45 km/h, with zero false positives on 110 real-world near-misses.
  • Deployment: risk perception learned from 113 real takeover events of experienced safety drivers in an L3 autonomous fleet; the model reproduces their intervention decisions with 92% accuracy and gives AVs a human-aligned risk threshold.
  • Li et al., Accident Analysis & Prevention, 2025
  • Sun et al., Accident Analysis & Prevention, 2026
  • Luo et al., Traffic Injury Prevention, 2026
